# Gospel

## Matthew 25:14-30
 
Jesus told his disciples this parable:  
“A man going on a journey  
   called in his servants and entrusted his possessions to them.  
To one he gave five talents; to another, two; to a third, one—  
   to each according to his ability.  
Then he went away.  
Immediately the one who received five talents went and traded with them,  
   and made another five.  
Likewise, the one who received two made another two.  
But the man who received one went off and dug a hole in the ground  
   and buried his master’s money.  
  
After a long time  
   the master of those servants came back  
   and settled accounts with them.  
The one who had received five talents came forward  
   bringing the additional five.  
He said, ‘Master, you gave me five talents.  
See, I have made five more.’  
His master said to him, ‘Well done, my good and faithful servant.  
Since you were faithful in small matters,  
   I will give you great responsibilities.  
Come, share your master’s joy.’  
Then the one who had received two talents also came forward and said,  
   ‘Master, you gave me two talents.  
See, I have made two more.’  
His master said to him, ‘Well done, my good and faithful servant.  
Since you were faithful in small matters,  
   I will give you great responsibilities.  
Come, share your master’s joy.’  
Then the one who had received the one talent came forward and said,  
   ‘Master, I knew you were a demanding person,  
   harvesting where you did not plant  
   and gathering where you did not scatter;  
   so out of fear I went off and buried your talent in the ground.  
Here it is back.’  
His master said to him in reply,‘You wicked, lazy servant!  
So you knew that I harvest where I did not plant  
   and gather where I did not scatter?  
Should you not then have put my money in the bank  
   so that I could have got it back with interest on my return?  
Now then! Take the talent from him and give it to the one with ten.  
For to everyone who has,  
   more will be given and he will grow rich;  
   but from the one who has not,  
   even what he has will be taken away.  
And throw this useless servant into the darkness outside,  
   where there will be wailing and grinding of teeth.’”



# Homily

- Life is full of unknowns and moments of uncertainty, whether it's changes at work, health concerns, or the general pace and pressures of the busy world we live in
	- Like the servants in the Gospel, or the early disciples looking toward an uncertain future, it is easy to let anxiety, fear of failure, resistance to change or the desire for comfort paralyze us

- I remember quite a few years ago at work, there was a project I was working on:    
    - We were in a situation with a lot of unknowns around the direction we should follow
    - Those unknowns, and the pressure of failing, were weighing heavily on the team, and we had stalled out, making very little progress
    - The project manager did something new that I had never experienced before: he booked what he called a FUD meeting with the whole team
    - At the start of the meeting, he explained that FUD stood for Fear, Uncertainty, and Doubt
    - The entire purpose of the meeting was to create a space for everyone to name the fears, uncertainties, and doubts we were carrying, putting everything openly on the table
    - Looking back, that single meeting completely turned the project around:
        - The energy of the team, our focus, and our productivity shifted from that point forward
    - My biggest takeaways from that experience were:
        - Voicing fears takes away their power: naming doubts openly defuses them and makes room for fresh ideas to move forward
        - You’re rarely alone: sharing uncertainties reveals that others are often wrestling with the exact same worries
        - Safe spaces build trust: giving people room to be honest helped us overcome our hesitation and created a stronger culture of trust
    - Interestingly enough, that went on to become one of the most successful projects I ever worked on

- In today's Gospel, we hear the Parable of the Talents:
    - Before going off on a long journey, the master entrusts his property to his servants "according to each one's ability"
    - To one, he entrusts five talents; to another, two; and to another, one talent, each portion uniquely suited to their own abilities
    - Those who received the five and two talents went to work, took risks, and put what they were given to use
    - Both doubled what was entrusted to them, and when they presented it back, the master commended them equally, calling them both "good and trustworthy"
    - They understood that love and faith require action and trust, even in the face of uncertainty and doubt
    - Facing uncertainty is never easy, and it is often uncomfortable, but there will always be seasons in our lives that call us to step outside our comfort zone

- The servant who received one talent took a very different path:
    - Let down by his own fear, uncertainty, and doubt, he dug a hole, hid the talent in the ground, and handed it back untouched when the master returned
    - He actually felt relieved returning that single talent, thinking he had fulfilled his duty simply by protecting it and avoiding loss
    - Yet the master was displeased
    - The talent was never meant to be locked away in safety
    - It was given by the master with generosity and confidence, meant to be risked, used, and allowed to bear fruit

- So how does this apply to us?
    - Each of us is entrusted with a set of talents uniquely our own, not just obvious skills, but our own particular way of seeing the world, our capacity for empathy, our quiet resilience, or our knack for bringing calm into a chaotic room
    - Yet, just like the servants in the Gospel, we are never handed a step-by-step instruction manual telling us exactly how to use them
    - God gives us genuine freedom, the freedom to choose how we invest our lives
    - We are meant to explore the gifts He has placed inside us: to test them out, stay curious about how they work, and notice where our deepest joys meet the world's real needs
    - We are often happiest when we are doing the things we were made to do, walking the path we were meant to follow
    - And just like the changing seasons remind us how trees spend an entire year quietly drawing in light and rain before revealing the brilliant colors they have carried all along, our own growth takes time, patience, and steady practice
    - You do not discover what your gifts can do by keeping them locked safely underground, you discover them by putting them to work

    - And yes, we will stumble and make mistakes along the way:
        - We will misjudge a situation, try something that isn't quite right, or fall short of what we had hoped to achieve
        - But even that is part of God’s design
        - we do not acquire wisdom by playing it safe
        - we learn wisdom through experience, by stepping out of our comfort zone and trying new things
    - Living this parable means giving ourselves permission to be beginners again:
        - To unearth whatever part of ourselves we have hidden out of fear of failure
        - To trust that God delights far more in our willingness to step into the light and try than in a spotless record of playing it safe

- So as we prepare to celebrate the Eucharist today, let us hold onto three simple invitations:
    - Pay attention to what gives you life: Often, the clearest sign of a God-given talent isn't what feels easiest, but what leaves you feeling deeply alive and connected when you offer it to someone else
    - Give yourself permission to experiment: You don’t need to have all the answers right now, just discern the very next step to explore a gift, and try that
    - Reframe past "failures" as learnings, not verdicts: If something you tried didn't turn out as planned, treat it as a season of growth rather than a reason to bury your gift permanently
